## Artefact 1: Authors, publishers sue Google over alleged AI copyright infringement

https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/15/authors-publishers-sue-google-over-alleged-ai-copyright-infringement

### Summary of the artefact

According to Hirschfeld (2026), Google faces a lawsuit from Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, and author Scott Turow, alleging it trained Gemini on books sourced from Google Books plus scraped and pirated material without permission. Internal documents reportedly flagged this as "highly problematic," risking $100 billion in fines.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

What made me choose this over a generic AI-copyright story is that the plaintiff is a textbook publisher, tying into the workshop's point that copyright protects a work's expression the moment it's created, software and databases included. I assumed AI training on scraped text sat in a legal grey area, but Google's own lawyers reportedly called it "highly problematic" first. This links to Artefact 4: who owns the output feels secondary to who chose to take that risk.

## Artefact 2: Federal judge rules Anthropic's AI training on books was fair use

https://www.npr.org/2025/06/25/nx-s1-5445242/federal-rules-in-ai-companys-favor-in-landmark-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-authors-bartz-graeber-wallace-johnson-anthropic

### Summary of the artefact

Veltman (2025) reports that authors Bartz, Graeber, and Johnson sued Anthropic for training Claude on copyrighted books without permission. Judge Alsup ruled that training on legally purchased books was fair use, but drew the line at pirated copies, now heading to trial with damages potentially reaching $150,000 per work.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this because it's the first real ruling to test the workshop's fair use four-factor doctrine against actual AI practice. The judgment split along how the books were obtained: fair use survived for purchased copies but not pirated ones, reshaping how I read Artefact 1, since Google's books came from "web-scraped and pirated material" too (Hirschfeld 2026).

## Artefact 3: Bridging the global gap in access to essential medicines

https://www.ohchr.org/en/stories/2025/07/bridging-global-gap-access-essential-medicines

### Summary of the artefact

Fyfe (2025), writing for the UN Human Rights Office, reports that around two billion people go without access to essential medicines, pointing to patent-driven pricing as a major cause, since a small commercial market gives little incentive to treat diseases affecting low-income populations.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this over another AI story to test the workshop's "limited-time compromise" idea against real people, not products. Society is meant to benefit once inventions eventually enter the public domain, but for two billion people without medicine, that "eventually" costs lives in the meantime. Unlike the other three, this one isn't about technology: it's about whose life a patent term is allowed to cost.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Personal Reflection

### Summary of the artefact

The Week 7 workshop opened with the monkey selfie case: does ownership belong to the monkey who pressed the shutter, or the photographer who owned the camera? The discussion turned to AI and asked the same question in a new form: does the prompter own the output, or the company running the data centres that generated it? The professor pointed out no dedicated AI copyright law exists yet, so AI-generated work currently sits outside copyright protection altogether.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

The idea I kept coming back to is accountability: when a prompt causes a bad outcome, the prompter gets held responsible, not the company, so credit for a good one should follow the same logic. Still, the company's compute is what let the idea get executed, so it probably deserves some share, not the whole product. This links to the workshop's Nike logo example: a watermark on AI output works like a logo, a branding signal, not an ownership claim. Ownership should stay with whoever made it.
