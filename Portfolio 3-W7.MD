## Artefact 1: Authors, publishers sue Google over alleged AI copyright infringement

https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/15/authors-publishers-sue-google-over-alleged-ai-copyright-infringement

### Summary of the artefact

According to Hirschfeld (2026), Google is facing a federal lawsuit brought by Hachette Book Group, Cengage Learning, Elsevier, and author Scott Turow, who allege the company trained its Gemini AI model on books sourced from Google Books plus scraped and pirated material, all without permission or payment. Fiction isn't the only concern raised in the complaint: it also covers "educational works and scholarly articles that span thousands of subject areas," and points to internal Google documents said to have flagged the practice as "highly problematic," warning it could expose the company to $100 billion in fines.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

What made me choose this over a generic AI-copyright story is that the plaintiff here is a textbook publisher, not just a novelist, which ties directly into Week 7's point that copyright covers a work's expression the moment it's created, computer programs and databases included (Galea 2026). Going in, I assumed AI training on scraped text sat in a grey area the law simply hadn't caught up with yet. That assumption didn't survive reading that Google's own lawyers had internally called the practice "highly problematic" before it went ahead regardless. This isn't an unsettled law being tested in good faith; it's a company weighing litigation against licensing and picking the cheaper option. It also feeds into the ownership question raised in Artefact 4: once a company can knowingly take that kind of risk with someone else's work and keep the resulting model anyway, "who owns the output" starts to feel like a secondary question next to who got to make that call in the first place.

## Artefact 2: Federal judge rules Anthropic's AI training on books was fair use

https://www.npr.org/2025/06/25/nx-s1-5445242/federal-rules-in-ai-companys-favor-in-landmark-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-authors-bartz-graeber-wallace-johnson-anthropic

### Summary of the artefact

Veltman (2025), writing for NPR, reports that authors Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson sued Anthropic, alleging the company trained its Claude AI models on millions of copyrighted books without permission. Senior US District Judge William Alsup ruled that training Claude on books Anthropic had legally purchased was fair use, calling it "exceedingly transformative." He drew a sharp line, though, at books Anthropic had downloaded for free from pirate sites: that conduct is not shielded by fair use and is proceeding to a December trial, with statutory damages potentially reaching $150,000 per pirated work across millions of titles.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this because it's the first real court ruling to test Week 7's fair use four-factor doctrine against actual AI training practice, rather than staying at the level of policy debate. Going in, I expected the ruling to land one of two ways: either training AI on books counts as fair use, or it doesn't. Instead the judgment split cleanly along how the books were obtained: transformative use was allowed for the copies Anthropic had actually purchased, but flatly rejected for the ones pulled from pirate sites. That changed how I read Artefact 1. Google's case involves books sourced through Google Books "alongside web-scraped and pirated material" (Hirschfeld 2026), and this ruling suggests its defence might survive for one slice of that and collapse for the other. Fair use, in other words, is not a single yes-or-no answer to "can AI train on copyrighted work": it is a factor-by-factor test that can rule in a company's favour on one count and against it on another, inside the same case.

## Artefact 3: Bridging the global gap in access to essential medicines

https://www.ohchr.org/en/stories/2025/07/bridging-global-gap-access-essential-medicines

### Summary of the artefact

Writing for the UN Human Rights Office, Fyfe (2025) reports that around two billion people worldwide go without access to essential medicines, and identifies patent-driven pricing as a major cause. The piece acknowledges that patents have genuinely funded medical innovation, but points out that they also leave diseases affecting low-income populations under-researched, since a small commercial market gives patent holders little reason to pursue treatments for them. Roughly 1.65 million people, concentrated in 16 low- and middle-income countries, currently need treatment for neglected tropical diseases.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I deliberately chose this over another AI story so I could test the "limited-time compromise" idea from Week 7 (Galea 2026) against real people rather than products. The workshop's case for IP protection rests on society benefiting once inventions eventually enter the public domain. For the two billion people currently without access to essential medicines, though, that "eventually" is quietly doing damage the entire time, potentially for someone's whole life. Of all four artefacts, this is the one that isn't really about technology at all: it's about whose life a patent term is allowed to cost. Setting it against Artefact 4's question of who deserves ownership and reward made me notice the same tension running underneath both: protection is meant to reward whoever creates value, but it rarely asks what that protection costs everyone else for as long as it lasts.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Personal Reflection

### Summary of the artefact

The Week 7 workshop kicked off with the monkey selfie case: does ownership of the photo belong to the monkey who pressed the shutter, or the photographer who owned the camera and set up the shot? From there the discussion turned to AI and asked the same question in a new form: does the person who wrote the prompt own the resulting output, or does the company running the data centres that generated it? Galea (2026) pointed out that no dedicated AI copyright law exists yet, so AI-generated work currently sits outside copyright protection altogether, meaning no one owns it in a legal sense.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

The idea I kept coming back to after class is accountability. When something goes wrong because of how someone prompted an AI, it's that person who gets held responsible, not the company behind the model. And if responsibility for a bad outcome falls on the prompter, credit for a good one should too, given that what's being rewarded is the idea and intent behind the prompt rather than the infrastructure that carried it out. That said, I don't think the logic holds all the way through: the idea couldn't have gone anywhere without the company's compute making execution possible, so legally the company probably deserves some share, a royalty or a partial benefit, rather than either the whole product or nothing at all. This links back to something covered later in the same workshop, the Nike logo example, where spotting the logo makes you think of the company and the shoe before anything else. I'd argue a watermark on AI output does something similar: a mark like the one NotebookLM adds to generated slides or videos signals the brand and flags that AI was involved, so a strong output reflects well on the company and a weak one reflects badly on it. That's a branding effect, though, not a claim to ownership. Ownership of what was actually produced should still belong to the person who made it.
